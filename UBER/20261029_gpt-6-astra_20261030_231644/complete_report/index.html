Let me open with the distinction that matters most: UBER has a weak stock-price trend, but its latest supplied financial statements show a substantially stronger earnings engine. Those are not the same thing.

The bearish chart, delivery competition, and declining cash balance deserve scrutiny. But the affirmative investment case is tangible: double-digit revenue growth, operating profit growing considerably faster than revenue, and approximately $10.1 billion in trailing free cash flow.

My position is bullish on UBER’s multi-year operating trajectory, with a phased, valuation-disciplined approach rather than an unconditional pre-earnings purchase.

1. The strongest evidence is already in the operating results¶

In the latest supplied financials, covering the first half of 2026:

  • Revenue increased 13.3% to $27.394 billion.
  • Operating income increased 42.4% to $3.813 billion.
  • Operating margin expanded from 11.07% to 13.92%.

That is meaningful operating leverage: UBER generated substantially more operating profit from each additional dollar of revenue.

Importantly, this argument does not depend on headline net income or an adjusted-earnings presentation. Reported operating income itself has improved materially.

A bearish case therefore needs to address an increasingly profitable business—not merely point out that competition exists.

2. UBER does not need extraordinary growth to produce attractive profit growth¶

The opportunity is broader than raising fares or increasing delivery fees. UBER can grow through more transaction occasions, higher customer frequency, cross-platform engagement, and more efficient marketplace matching.

Its competitive advantages are structural, although not invulnerable:

  • Marketplace density: More available drivers and couriers can improve matching, availability, and utilization.
  • Brand and distribution: An established customer relationship can support repeat usage and cross-selling.
  • Multiple demand engines: Mobility and Delivery give UBER more transaction opportunities than a single-category platform.

These are potential advantages—not proof that acquisition costs are falling or that every segment is gaining share. Customers and providers can use competing platforms, and the supplied extracts lack the segment metrics needed to measure those advantages directly.

Still, the financial results show that the overall model has been scaling profitably. First-half reported capital expenditure was just $135 million, approximately 0.5% of revenue. That supports the scalability argument, although capex does not capture every economic investment or cash commitment.
