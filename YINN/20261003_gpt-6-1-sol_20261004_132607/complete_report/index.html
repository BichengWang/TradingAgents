7. YINN-specific risk considerations¶

YINN seeks approximately three times its benchmark’s daily return, before fees and expenses. It should not be assumed to deliver three times the benchmark’s cumulative return over a longer holding period. Daily resetting, compounding, and the path of returns can materially change outcomes.

For risk planning:

  • Size from permitted loss, not desired exposure. A basic framework is: Shares ≈ permitted dollar risk ÷ (entry-to-stop distance + slippage allowance).
  • Do not treat the latest low as an automatic swing-trading stop. From the verified close, 22.88 is only 0.29 lower, much less than the current ATR.
  • A wider stop requires smaller exposure. A hypothetical two-ATR distance is approximately 2.12 per share using the rounded verified ATR. That is a sizing illustration, not a recommended stop or established support level.
  • Allow for gap risk. Stop orders cannot guarantee the intended exit price, particularly when relevant China-market developments occur outside YINN’s U.S. trading session.

These tools do not establish current spreads, execution quality, options pricing, macro catalysts, or investor suitability. The final trade and allocation decision therefore requires information beyond this technical report.

4. YINN’s most important quantitative feature: daily compounding¶

The following is a mechanical illustration, not observed YINN performance.

[ R_{\text{YINN},t} \approx 3R_{\text{benchmark},t} ]

Over several days, the simplified cumulative result is:

[ R_{\text{YINN,cumulative}} \approx \prod_t(1+3R_{\text{benchmark},t})-1 ]

It is not generally three times the benchmark’s cumulative return.

A flat benchmark can still produce a YINN loss¶

Suppose the benchmark:

  • Rises 10% on the first day.
  • Falls 9.0909% on the second day.

The benchmark finishes approximately unchanged:

A hypothetical fund achieving exactly three times each daily move would return:

[ 1.30 \times 0.727273 - 1 \approx -5.45\% ]

That loss occurs before fees and financing costs.

Compounding is not always adverse: sustained directional moves can produce favorable compounding. The key point is that the sequence of returns matters, not just the starting and ending benchmark levels.
